Most small importers learn HS code classification the same way: they pick a 6-digit code that looks right, the freight forwarder uses it, and either the shipment clears or it gets held at the border while customs reclassifies it — usually upward by 4–8 percent of duty. Getting the code wrong is the most expensive easy mistake in cross-border trade.

The customs-and-trade skill takes a product description, a BOM, or a HS suggestion and validates it against the destination country's tariff schedule (EU TARIC, US HTSUS, UK Global Tariff, China MFN). It then calculates the duty rate, applies any preferential-origin reduction (USMCA, EUR.1, REX, GSP), estimates the landed cost, and screens the importer and consignee against EU + US + UK sanctions lists.

Why it matters

A wrong HS code does three things: it pays the wrong duty (usually too low, then back-billed with interest), it triggers a customs inspection that holds the container for 5–10 days, and it can flag the shipment for fraud review if customs concludes the misclassification was intentional. None of those are good for a small brand on its third container.

The right HS code does the opposite. It lets you predict duty and landed cost before you commit to the shipment. It lets you check whether your product qualifies for a preferential trade agreement that reduces duty to zero. And it surfaces dual-use or export-controlled items before they trigger an ITAR or EAR violation.

The skill ties HS classification to the actual destination tariff schedule — EU TARIC at 10 digits, US HTSUS at 10 digits, UK Global Tariff post-Brexit, China MFN at 8 digits — so the duty number you get is the duty you will actually pay, not a generic ad-valorem estimate.

How the customs-and-trade skill works

  1. Describe the product. Give the agent the product description, materials, function and use-case. The more specific, the more accurate the classification — especially for textiles, prepared foods, and machinery.
  2. Classify to the destination's tariff schedule. The skill pulls EU TARIC, US HTSUS, UK GT, China MFN and proposes the 8–10 digit code with the WCO Explanatory Notes cited. It flags alternative codes and explains why each was rejected.
  3. Calculate duty and VAT/sales tax. Apply the MFN duty rate, then any preferential origin (USMCA, EUR.1, REX, GSP). Add destination VAT or sales tax, anti-dumping duty if applicable.
  4. Estimate landed cost. Combine ex-works price, freight (sea or air), insurance, duty, VAT, customs broker fee, and last-mile delivery for the destination warehouse.
  5. Screen for dual-use and sanctions. Check the 10-digit code against EU dual-use list, US EAR Commerce Control List, ITAR USML. Screen consignee against EU + OFAC + UK sanctions lists.
  6. Generate the customs docs. Commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin (EUR.1, REX statement, USMCA certification), dangerous goods declaration if applicable, Incoterms 2020 responsibility matrix.

FAQ

What is an HS code?

Harmonized System code — a 6-digit international product classification maintained by the World Customs Organization, used by 200+ countries to assign duty rates. Most countries extend it to 8 or 10 digits for national tariff lines (EU TARIC = 10, US HTSUS = 10, China MFN = 8).

Why does the wrong HS code matter?

It causes the wrong duty to be paid (usually back-billed with interest), triggers customs inspection delays of 5–10 days, and in some jurisdictions can be treated as customs fraud if intentional. It also affects free-trade agreement eligibility.

Can the skill find a preferential rate under USMCA or EUR.1?

Yes. The skill checks the product against the rules of origin in each FTA (e.g. USMCA Article 4.2, EU CETA Annex 5) and confirms whether the product qualifies. It then generates the correct origin statement language.

Does it check for sanctions and dual-use?

Yes. Consignee and vessel are screened against EU consolidated list, OFAC SDN, UK HM Treasury sanctions, and the destination's domestic equivalents. The HS code is matched against the EU dual-use list (Reg 2021/821), the US EAR Commerce Control List, and ITAR USML where applicable.

Does it replace a customs broker?

No. A licensed customs broker is required for actual border clearance in most jurisdictions. The skill prepares the classification, duty calculation, and docs so the broker has accurate inputs — cutting broker time and reducing surprise duty bills.
